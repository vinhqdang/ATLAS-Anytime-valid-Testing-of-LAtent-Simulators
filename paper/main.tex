\title{ATLAS: Anytime-Valid Certification of World-Model Faithfulness by Betting}

\author{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
A world model is useful only while its imagined rollouts stay close to what the environment
would have done, and that closeness depends on the rollout horizon and changes during deployment.
Offline scores such as Fr\'echet Video Distance or held-out return gaps are computed once and
cannot follow either effect. We study how to monitor and certify a deployed world model
sequentially, with error guarantees that hold uniformly over time and that remain valid when
the planner's own actions, chosen through the model, determine which states the monitor sees.
ATLAS bets against the null ``horizon $h$ is $\eps$-faithful'' with a test martingale for each
horizon, and uses the same betting machinery in estimation mode to produce confidence sequences
on the faithfulness gap. The first object, the \emph{revocation frontier}, controls false alarms;
the second, the \emph{certified horizon}, controls the opposite error, trusting a horizon that
has drifted, which is what a planner gating its imagination depth needs. We prove time-uniform
validity, a corrected confidence sequence for the running average of conditional means with a
simulation-lemma-style consequence for per-horizon total variation, and a finite-sample detection
delay bound with a horizon-resolved growth rate. On a simulator with known ground truth the delay law predicts measured delays within $16\%$ across horizons and the
certified bound is never violated. On a closed-loop stream where a model-based planner steers the state, CUSUM and a conformal martingale monitoring raw prediction error
alarm in every run when the planner heads for high-noise states, and every raw-error monitor reaches false-alarm rates of $0.40$--$0.73$ under a monitor-aware planner, while ATLAS stays at or below $0.013$.
On three real video shifts, replicated over 30 random splits and compared with standard change detectors on identical streams, ATLAS detects every shift but needs a tolerance margin
to keep false alarms near the nominal level. We also report negative results: pooling overlapping horizon chains does not recover the data they discard, and calibrating the tolerance on pooled data voids the conditional guarantee.
\end{abstract}
